# Diagnostic playbook — box-tree moth intervention

Box-tree moth (*Cydalima perspectalis*) is now endemic across DE-BW
and is the single most serious threat to common-box (*Buxus
sempervirens*) hedges and topiary in Stuttgart gardens. The
caterpillars defoliate plants from the inside out and can strip a
mature plant in 2-3 weeks if left unmanaged. Early detection through
pheromone-trap monitoring is what separates a manageable problem from
a destroyed hedge.

## Pheromone-trap rhythm

Three generations per year in the Stuttgart climate:

- **First generation** — emerges late April / early May. Place traps
  in late April.
- **Second generation** — emerges mid- to late June. Refresh
  pheromone capsules.
- **Third generation** — emerges mid- to late August. Refresh capsules.

Pheromone traps are **for monitoring, not control**. Trap catches tell
you when to inspect the plant intensively, not that the trap is
preventing damage. A box hedge with no trap catches at all in the
relevant flight window should still be inspected weekly — the traps
sometimes miss localised populations.

## Visual indicators of active infestation

- **Webbing** — fine silk webbing inside the canopy, especially in the
  upper third of the plant. Often visible from below, looking up.
- **Caterpillars** — green with black-and-white stripes along the body,
  reaching about 4 cm at the largest instar. Initially small and well
  camouflaged, especially against the inside of the foliage.
- **Frass** (caterpillar droppings) — small dark green or black
  granules on lower leaves and on the ground beneath the plant. The
  most reliable early sign.
- **Leaf damage** — chewed, brown, papery leaves on the upper inner
  canopy. The outer leaves can look healthy long after the centre is
  stripped.

## Differential diagnosis

- **Boxwood blight** — circular brown leaf spots and stem streaking,
  not chewed leaves and no webbing. A fungus, not an insect. (See
  boxwood plant-care guide.)
- **Volutella blight** — dieback of individual branches with salmon-pink
  fruiting bodies on stems. Different organism, different treatment.
- **Drought stress / winter burn** — uniform browning on the outer
  canopy, no webbing and no frass.

## Intervention

If caterpillars or webbing are confirmed:

1. **Strong water jet** — knock off as many caterpillars as possible
   using a hose. They drop to the ground and are eaten by birds and
   ground beetles. This alone reduces a moderate infestation
   substantially.
2. **Biological control** — Bacillus thuringiensis subspecies kurstaki
   (Bt-k) is the standard intervention. Apply in the late afternoon
   (Bt-k is sensitive to UV) on a dry day with no rain forecast for
   24 hours. Spray to thoroughly coat the *interior* of the canopy —
   the outer surface is not where the caterpillars feed. A second
   application 7-10 days later catches caterpillars that hatched after
   the first spray.
3. **Manual removal** for small or topiary plants — slow but effective.
   Wear gloves; caterpillars are non-toxic but can cause skin
   irritation in sensitive individuals.

## Pesticide last resort

If biological control fails to break the cycle after two seasons, a
contact-insecticide application is the next step — but recurrence is
nearly guaranteed once a population is established in the area. For
many homeowners, replacing boxwood with Japanese holly (*Ilex
crenata*) or sweet box (*Sarcococca confusa*) is the more durable
long-term answer than continuous spraying.

## Confidence cue for the vision model

A photograph showing webbing, frass, or visible caterpillars is a
high-confidence identification. **Photographs of just damaged leaves
without webbing or caterpillars** are much harder to distinguish from
boxwood blight and should prompt the "second opinion" suggestion —
the treatments are completely different.

---

*This playbook is advisory only. Boxwood-moth populations vary
locally; if intervention fails to control the population over two
seasons, consult a local nursery or arborist about alternative plant
species.*
