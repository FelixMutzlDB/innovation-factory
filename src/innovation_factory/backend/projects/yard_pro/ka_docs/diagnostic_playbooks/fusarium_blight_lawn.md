# Diagnostic playbook — fusarium blight on lawns

Fusarium blight (often *Microdochium nivale*, sometimes called pink
snow mould or fusarium patch) is the most common fungal disease of
residential lawns in the Stuttgart region during the cool, damp
shoulder seasons (March-April and October-November). It produces
circular yellowing patches on the lawn that can expand and merge if
ignored.

## Visual indicators

- **Patch shape:** roughly circular, 5-30 cm across initially. Patches
  can merge into irregular larger areas over weeks if untreated.
- **Patch colour:** patches start as yellow-tan, then turn straw-brown
  in the centre as the grass dies. The outer edge can show a faint
  pink-orange tinge in cool, damp morning conditions — this is the
  fungal mycelium itself.
- **Surrounding grass:** healthy green, with no transition zone — the
  edge between damaged and healthy grass is sharp.
- **Underlying soil:** usually moist, slightly compacted, with mat or
  thatch buildup above.

## Differential diagnosis — what fusarium is NOT

- **Drought stress** — patches are irregular, follow soil contours, and
  recover with watering. Fusarium patches do not.
- **Dog urine spots** — small (10-20 cm), bright yellow centre with a
  dark green ring of accelerated growth. Fusarium has no such ring.
- **Red thread disease** — visible pink-red "threads" of fungal growth
  on the blades themselves, more like fine red hairs. A different
  fungus; less aggressive than fusarium.
- **Take-all patch** — irregular sunken bronze patches with thinning
  edges. Less common in Stuttgart.

## Treatment

The first-line interventions are cultural rather than chemical:

1. **Reduce moisture at the surface.** Switch from daily shallow
   watering to a deep weekly soak. Water in the early morning only —
   wet blades overnight feed the fungus.
2. **Raise the mowing height** to 5 cm in shoulder seasons, never below
   4 cm. Short turf is most susceptible.
3. **Scarify and overseed** affected patches in late March or early
   September. Scarifying removes the thatch layer where fusarium
   over-summers, and the overseed restores grass cover.
4. **Reduce nitrogen on fungus-prone lawns in autumn.** Switch to a
   high-potassium autumn fertilizer (NPK ~6-5-20), which hardens the
   grass without driving the soft growth that fusarium prefers.

Chemical fungicides for fusarium are available but are typically
restricted to professional turf care; a residential lawn is almost
always recoverable through the cultural measures above.

## Recovery timeline

After cultural intervention, expect 4-6 weeks of visible patches
before the overseed germinates and fills in. A scarified-and-overseeded
patch in early April is typically fully closed by late May.

## Confidence cue for the vision model

A clear photograph of a fusarium-blight patch (taken with the patch
filling the frame, in good light, including the sharp transition to
healthy grass) is one of the more reliable identifications. Low
confidence on a "yellow patch in lawn" call should always prompt a
second-opinion suggestion — the most common confounders are drought
and dog urine, neither of which needs fungicide intervention.

---

*This playbook is advisory only. If patches expand rapidly despite
cultural intervention, or if the lawn shows multiple disease types
simultaneously, consult a local turf-care specialist.*
